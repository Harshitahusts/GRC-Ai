The DPDP Rules 2025 turn India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 from principles into practice. Notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) in **November 2025**, they set out how notices must look, what "reasonable security" means, how fast breaches must be reported, and when the obligations start.

Here's what changes for businesses, and when.

## When the DPDP Rules 2025 take effect

The Rules come into force in three phases:

- **November 2025**: provisions setting up the **Data Protection Board** take effect immediately.
- **About 12 months later**: rules for registering **Consent Managers** take effect.
- **About 18 months later, 13 May 2027**: the **main obligations** take effect, covering notice, consent, security, breach reporting, erasure, children's data and individual rights.

That 18-month runway is the window to get compliant. See the full [DPDPA timeline](/docs/dpdpa-timeline).

## 1. Notices must stand on their own

The Rules require the consent notice to be understandable **independently** of other information, in clear and plain language, with an **itemised description** of the personal data and the **specific purpose** for each use. It must include a way to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board.

A privacy policy linked in the footer no longer does the job on its own. See [DPDPA notice requirements](/docs/dpdpa-notice-requirements).

## 2. "Reasonable security safeguards" now has a floor

Section 8(5) of the Act requires reasonable security safeguards. The Rules list the minimum:

- encryption, obfuscation, masking or virtual tokens for personal data
- access control on the systems that hold it
- visibility of access, through logs, monitoring and review, and keeping logs so incidents can be investigated
- backups and measures to continue processing after an incident
- matching security obligations in contracts with Data Processors

Failing here carries the Act's biggest penalty: up to **₹250 crore**. See [Data Fiduciary obligations](/docs/dpdpa-data-fiduciary-obligations).

## 3. Breach reports: without delay, then within 72 hours

The Rules set a two-step process. Tell each affected person **without delay**, explaining what happened, the likely consequences and what they can do. Tell the Data Protection Board **without delay**, then send a **detailed report within 72 hours** covering the cause, the measures taken and the notices sent.

There's no minimum size for a reportable breach. See [personal data breach reporting](/docs/dpdpa-personal-data-breach).

## 4. Data can't be kept forever

The Act requires erasure once the purpose is served. For certain large platforms (e-commerce, online gaming and social media intermediaries above set user thresholds), the Rules set a specific period after which inactive users' data must be erased, with advance notice to the user. Every business should set retention periods and delete data it no longer needs.

## 5. Verifiable consent for children

Before processing a child's data, a business must verify that the person consenting is an identifiable adult, using reliable identity and age details it already has, or a token from an authorised provider such as DigiLocker. Some processing, such as by schools and healthcare providers for specific purposes, is exempt. See [children's data](/docs/dpdpa-childrens-data).

## 6. Annual audits for Significant Data Fiduciaries

Organisations notified as **Significant Data Fiduciaries** must carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment and an audit **every 12 months**, check their algorithms for risks to users' rights, and may have to keep certain data in India. See [Significant Data Fiduciaries](/docs/dpdpa-significant-data-fiduciary).

## 7. Rights requests and grievances need a process

Businesses must publish how people can exercise their rights and raise grievances, and respond to grievances within a set period of up to 90 days. See [Data Principal rights](/docs/dpdpa-data-principal-rights).

## What to do now

1. Map your personal data, systems and vendors.
2. Rewrite your consent notices to the new format.
3. Check your security measures against the minimum list.
4. Write a breach playbook with the 72-hour Board report in it.
5. Set retention periods and a deletion routine.
